\begin{abstract}
Matching two unrelated views and tracking features between consecutive video frames
call for different pipelines. We built both kinds in the Lab~5 stencil (ROS Noetic,
OpenCV~3.4.2 with contrib): SIFT, SURF, ORB and FAST+BRIEF trackers with $k$NN
matching, Lowe's ratio test and RANSAC; a Harris + pyramidal Lucas--Kanade tracker; and
Farneback dense optical flow. On the \code{box} image pair, where the box is rotated and
about half as large in the scene, scale-invariant descriptors win: SURF finds the most
inliers (88), SIFT reproduces the handout (603/969 keypoints, 79 inliers, 79\,\% inlier
ratio), and ORB matches well once its scale pyramid is enabled. On the two video
datasets every method keeps more than 84\,\% of its matches as inliers, and Harris+LK
leads on both reliability (95.5--99.7\,\% inliers) and speed (3.0--5.9\,ms per frame
pair). Among the descriptor methods, ORB gives the best trade-off for onboard visual
odometry: 98.1\,\% inliers on the real data at 9--12\,ms per pair, against 64--145\,ms
for SIFT.
\end{abstract}


\section{Individual work}

Throughout this section the camera has principal point $(0,0)$, focal length $1$,
$s_x=s_y=1$ and $s_\theta=0$, so a point $p=[x\ y\ z]^\top$ with $z>0$ projects to
$(u,v)=(x/z,\,y/z)$, and the pixel $(u,v)$ corresponds to the ray of points
$\lambda w$, $\lambda>0$, with $w=[u\ v\ 1]^\top$.

\subsection{Deliverable 1: Perspective projection of a sphere}

\paragraph{1. Sphere on the optical axis.}
Let the centre be $c=[0\ 0\ d]^\top$. Because $d>r+1$, every point of the sphere has
$z\ge d-r>1$, so the sphere lies entirely in front of the image plane $z=1$ and is fully
visible. A pixel $(u,v)$ belongs to the image of the sphere iff its ray $\{\lambda w\}$
meets the sphere, i.e.\ iff the distance from $c$ to the line through the origin with
direction $w$ is at most $r$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\lVert c\times w\rVert^2}{\lVert w\rVert^2}\le r^2 .
\end{equation}
The boundary of the image (the \emph{occluding contour}) is formed by the rays tangent
to the sphere, where equality holds. With $c\times w=[-dv\ \ du\ \ 0]^\top$,
$\lVert c\times w\rVert^2=d^2(u^2+v^2)$ and $\lVert w\rVert^2=u^2+v^2+1$, hence
\begin{equation}
  d^2(u^2+v^2)=r^2(u^2+v^2+1)
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \boxed{\,u^2+v^2=\frac{r^2}{d^2-r^2}\,}.
\end{equation}
The projection is a disc centred at the principal point, bounded by a circle of radius
$\rho=r/\sqrt{d^2-r^2}$ (well defined since $d>r$).
Geometrically, the tangent rays form a right circular cone with apex at the optical
centre, axis along the optical axis and half-angle $\alpha$ with $\sin\alpha=r/d$. The
image plane is perpendicular to the axis and cuts the cone in a circle of radius
$\tan\alpha=r/\sqrt{d^2-r^2}$. For $d\gg r$ this tends to $r/d$, the familiar
``size divided by depth'' rule.

\paragraph{2. Sphere at an arbitrary position.}
For a general centre $c=[c_x\ c_y\ c_z]^\top$, the identity
$\lVert c\times w\rVert^2=\lVert c\rVert^2\lVert w\rVert^2-(c^\top w)^2$ turns the
tangency condition $\lVert c\times w\rVert^2=r^2\lVert w\rVert^2$ into
\begin{equation}
  (c_x u+c_y v+c_z)^2=k\,(u^2+v^2+1),\qquad k=\lVert c\rVert^2-r^2 .
  \label{eq:conic}
\end{equation}
This equation is quadratic in $(u,v)$, so the contour is always a \textbf{conic}. It is
the intersection of the tangent cone (still a right circular cone, now with its axis
along $c$) with an image plane that is no longer perpendicular to that axis.
Rotating the image axes so that $u'$ points along $(c_x,c_y)$, writing
$\rho_c=\sqrt{c_x^2+c_y^2}$ and completing the square turns \eqref{eq:conic} into
\begin{equation}
  (c_z^2-r^2)\Bigl(u'-\frac{\rho_c c_z}{c_z^2-r^2}\Bigr)^2+k\,v'^2=\frac{r^2k}{c_z^2-r^2}.
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
  \item If the sphere is fully in front of the camera ($c_z>r$, hence $k>0$), both
    coefficients are positive and the projection is an \textbf{ellipse} centred at
    $u'_0=\rho_c c_z/(c_z^2-r^2)$, $v'_0=0$, with semi-axes
    \[a_{\text{radial}}=\frac{r\sqrt{\lVert c\rVert^2-r^2}}{c_z^2-r^2},\qquad
      a_{\perp}=\frac{r}{\sqrt{c_z^2-r^2}} .\]
    The major axis points radially away from the principal point, and the elongation
    $a_{\text{radial}}/a_\perp=\sqrt{(\lVert c\rVert^2-r^2)/(c_z^2-r^2)}\ge 1$ grows
    as the sphere moves towards the border of the field of view. The centre of the
    ellipse is shifted outwards from $\rho_c/c_z$, the projection of the sphere centre.
    For $c_x=c_y=0$ we recover the circle of part~1.
  \item If the sphere touches the plane $z=0$ through the optical centre ($c_z=r$), the
    $u'^2$ coefficient vanishes and the contour is a \textbf{parabola}. If it crosses
    that plane ($|c_z|<r$, camera still outside the sphere), the coefficients have
    opposite signs and the contour is (one branch of) a \textbf{hyperbola}. In both
    cases part of the sphere is behind the camera.
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{fig:sphere} checks these formulas numerically. We project $2\times10^5$
points sampled on a unit sphere at depth $c_z=5$; for $c_x=0,\dots,10$ the extents of the
projected points match the analytic ellipse to within $4\times10^{-6}$. The ellipse
stretches radially as the sphere moves off axis ($a_{\text{radial}}/a_\perp=2.27$ at
$63^\circ$), and its centre lies further out than the projected sphere centre.

\subsection{Deliverable 2: Vanishing points}

\paragraph{1. Vanishing point of a family of parallel lines.}
Write a 3D line as $p(\lambda)=p_0+\lambda u$ with $\lVert u\rVert=1$. Its image is
\begin{equation}
  \pi(p(\lambda))=\Bigl(\frac{p_{0,x}+\lambda u_x}{p_{0,z}+\lambda u_z},\
                        \frac{p_{0,y}+\lambda u_y}{p_{0,z}+\lambda u_z}\Bigr).
\end{equation}
If $u_z\neq0$, letting $\lambda\to\infty$ in the direction in which the line moves away
from the camera ($\lambda u_z>0$) gives
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\,v_\infty=\Bigl(\frac{u_x}{u_z},\ \frac{u_y}{u_z}\Bigr)\,},
\end{equation}
which depends only on the direction $u$ and not on the point $p_0$. Every line with
direction $u$, in particular both parallel lines $p_0+\lambda u$ and $q_0+\mu u$,
therefore passes through $v_\infty$ in the image: $v_\infty$ is the vanishing point. It
is the image of the point at infinity $[u^\top\ 0]^\top$, i.e.\ the intersection of the
image plane with the ray through the optical centre parallel to the lines. With a
general calibration matrix $K$ it reads $v_\infty\simeq Ku$ in homogeneous coordinates.

\emph{Geometric view.} Each 3D line spans, together with the optical centre $O$, a plane
whose intersection with the image plane is the image line. For two parallel lines both
planes contain the direction $u$, so they intersect along the line through $O$ with
direction $u$, which hits the image plane exactly at $v_\infty$.

\paragraph{2. When do parallel lines stay parallel?}
\textbf{Claim:} the images of two distinct parallel 3D lines (neither through $O$) are
parallel if and only if their direction is parallel to the image plane, $u_z=0$.

\emph{Proof.} ($\Leftarrow$) If $u_z=0$ the depth along the line is constant,
$z=p_{0,z}$, and
$\pi(p(\lambda))=\frac{1}{p_{0,z}}[p_{0,x}\ p_{0,y}]^\top+\frac{\lambda}{p_{0,z}}[u_x\ u_y]^\top$:
the image is a line with direction $[u_x\ u_y]^\top$. The second line has the same image
direction, so the two image lines are parallel (the vanishing point $Ku$ has third
coordinate $0$ and lies at infinity).
($\Rightarrow$) If $u_z\neq0$, both image lines contain the finite point $v_\infty$ from
part~1. They intersect there and cannot be parallel; they can only coincide, when the
two 3D lines are coplanar with $O$. $\square$

In the geometric picture, the two planes through $O$ meet in the line through $O$ with
direction $u$, and this line meets the image plane at a finite point unless it is
parallel to it ($u_z=0$). For a camera looking straight at a wall, for example, the
horizontal and vertical edges on the wall stay parallel in the image, while edges
receding in depth converge. Figure~\ref{fig:vp} projects five parallel lines with
different starting points for both cases.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{d2_vanishing_points.pdf}
  \caption{Images of five parallel 3D lines (dots: projected starting points $p_0$).
    (a)~With $u_z\neq0$ all images converge to $v_\infty=(1,\,0.5)$. (b)~With $u_z=0$
    they keep the image direction $[u_x\ u_y]^\top$ and stay parallel.}
  \label{fig:vp}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Implementation and setup}

\paragraph{Build.} The official 2023 ROS1 stencil (\code{feature\_tracking}, package
\code{lab\_5}) is built in a catkin workspace together with \code{opencv3\_catkin}
(OpenCV 3.4.2 + contrib, whose \code{xfeatures2d} module still contains the patented
SIFT and SURF). Relative to the official package we switched off modules the lab does
not use (Python bindings, VTK/viz, FFMPEG) and use the system glog instead of
\code{glog\_catkin}. Because \code{cv\_bridge} exports the system OpenCV~4.2 libraries,
we also filter them out of the link line of \code{lab\_5}; otherwise two
\code{highgui} versions (GTK2 and GTK3) are loaded and \code{imshow} aborts.
\code{scripts/build\_workspace.sh} builds everything (OpenCV takes 4~min on 12 cores).

\paragraph{Descriptor-based trackers (D3--D6).} We compare SIFT~\cite{lowe2004sift},
SURF~\cite{bay2006surf}, ORB~\cite{rublee2011orb} and FAST~\cite{rosten2006fast} +
BRIEF~\cite{calonder2010brief}.
\code{FeatureTracker::trackFeatures} detects keypoints in both images
(\code{detector->detect}), describes them (\code{detector->compute}; FAST uses a
separate \code{BriefDescriptorExtractor}), and calls \code{matchDescriptors}. That
function runs \code{knnMatch} with $k=2$ and keeps \code{m[0]} if
\code{m[0].distance < 0.8 m[1].distance}; queries with fewer than two neighbours are
dropped. SIFT and SURF use the default \code{FlannBasedMatcher} (randomised kd-trees on
float descriptors~\cite{muja2009flann}). ORB and BRIEF descriptors are binary and are
matched with a brute-force Hamming matcher (\code{BFMatcher(NORM\_HAMMING)}); a
parameter switches to a FLANN LSH index, compared below.
The good matches go to the provided \code{inlierMaskComputation} (fundamental matrix
with RANSAC, 3\,px epipolar threshold), and two \code{cv::drawMatches} calls
(\code{DRAW\_OVER\_OUTIMG}) draw the inliers in green over the red outliers. The
provided code averages all statistics, and we also log them per frame to CSV.

\paragraph{Two fixes to the stencil.} In video mode, \code{imageCallback} stored
\code{prev\_image = image}, where \code{image} comes from \code{cv\_bridge::toCvShare} and
does not own its pixels; we keep a \code{clone()} instead. The stencil also matched the
first frame with itself (100\,\% inliers); we now skip it.

\paragraph{Statistics.} As in the handout table, ``\# of Matches'' is the number of
$k$NN queries (one per keypoint of image~1), ``Good Matches'' are those that pass the
ratio test, and the inlier ratio is $\#\text{inliers}/\#\text{good matches}$. We add the
wall-clock time of detection, description and matching for one image pair (both images
processed, drawing excluded; Intel Core i5-12400F, Release build).

Four parameter choices explain the differences.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{ORB needs its scale pyramid.} The stencil's
    \code{ORB::create(500, 1.2, 1)} uses one pyramid level to avoid the ``Unknown
    interpolation method'' error mentioned in the handout, which makes ORB single-scale
    and leaves 7 inliers. OpenCV~3.4.2 runs the 8-level pyramid without that error and
    gives 26 inliers (89.7\,\% ratio); with 2000 features ORB reaches 55 inliers at
    19\,ms, a third of the SIFT and SURF time.
  \item \textbf{The default SURF Hessian threshold (100) gives the most inliers.} Raising
    it to 400 saves 25\,\% of the time but drops the inliers from 88 to 76.
\end{itemize}
\begin{keep}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{FAST+BRIEF is built for small viewpoint changes.} Neither FAST nor BRIEF
    is scale invariant and BRIEF is not rotation invariant, so this pair leaves it 41 good
    matches and 17 inliers; raising the FAST threshold from 10 to 30 removes good matches
    faster than corners. With so few correct matches, RANSAC also accepts some wrong ones
    (Fig.~\ref{fig:pairgrid}), because the fundamental-matrix test only checks the
    distance to an epipolar line.
\end{itemize}

  \item \textbf{Brute-force Hamming beats FLANN-LSH for binary descriptors.}

    is rebuilt for every image pair and the descriptor sets are small. Brute-force
    Hamming distance is a XOR and a popcount per pair.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Conclusions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Consecutive frames are much easier than the image pair.} Between two
    video frames the image moves by a few pixels with almost no scale or rotation change,
    so all methods reach 84--98\,\% inlier ratio, including FAST+BRIEF and single-level
    ORB. Invariance to large scale and rotation changes (SIFT, SURF, multi-scale ORB)
    pays off when matching unrelated views, as in loop closure, more than in
    frame-to-frame tracking.
  \item \textbf{Keypoint counts follow the thresholds and the scene.} SURF finds by far
    the most keypoints on the simulated images (1194) and SIFT the fewest (270, with the
    contrast threshold already halved); on the real images FAST and ORB find the most.
    Lowering a threshold raises the number of inliers almost proportionally at a small
    cost in inlier ratio.
\end{itemize}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Binary descriptors keep more of their matches.} On the real dataset
    FAST+BRIEF keeps 77\,\% and ORB 74\,\% of the $k$NN matches after the ratio test,
    against 56\,\% for SIFT, and ORB has the highest inlier ratio of the descriptor
    methods (98.1\,\%). On the simulated dataset all ratios are lower (84--91\,\%):
    large textureless walls and repeated structures (identical gates, floor tiles) give
    ambiguous descriptors, and Fig.~\ref{fig:perframe} shows the ratio dropping when the
    camera faces such regions or turns quickly.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Deliverable 7: Harris corners + Lucas--Kanade tracker}\label{sec:lk}

\paragraph{Implementation.} We combine Harris corners~\cite{harris1988corner} with the
Lucas--Kanade tracker~\cite{lucas1981lk}. Our \code{trackFeatures} converts the frame to
grayscale and runs \code{calcOpticalFlowPyrLK} ($21\times21$ window, 4 pyramid
levels, at most 30 iterations / $\varepsilon=0.01$) from the corners of the previous
frame. Corners with \code{status == 1} are the matches, and the provided
\code{inlierMaskComputation} (fundamental matrix + RANSAC) selects the inliers. Only the
inliers are tracked further. Every frame, \code{goodFeaturesToTrack}
(\code{useHarrisDetector = true}, $k=0.04$, quality level 0.01) tops up the set with new
Harris corners, masked to stay at least the minimum distance away from the corners
already tracked. \code{show()} draws the last 20 positions of every track in green and
the RANSAC outliers in red (Fig.~\ref{fig:lk}; videos in
\code{video/d7\_lk\_tracking\_*.mp4}). For the statistics, ``Keypoints in Img~1'' are the
corners LK tries to track, ``Keypoints in Img~2'' the corners available in the current
frame after re-detection, ``Matches'' counts one query per corner (as for the descriptor
methods), and ``Good matches'' are corners with \code{status == 1}. The results are in
the last column of Tables~\ref{tab:30fps}--\ref{tab:smooth}.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering

    \caption{\code{30fps\_424x240}, frame 150.}\end{subfigure}\hfill
  \begin{subfigure}{0.58\linewidth}\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{d7_smooth_lk_tracks.jpg}
    \caption{\code{smooth-trajectory}, frame 150.}\end{subfigure}

The average number of corners stays below the maximum (561 of 1000 on the real data, 276
on the simulated data) because \code{goodFeaturesToTrack} only accepts corners whose
Harris response exceeds 1\,\% of the strongest corner in the image.

\paragraph{Assumptions of the LK tracker.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Brightness constancy:} a feature keeps the same intensity between
    frames, $I(x+\delta x, y+\delta y, t+\delta t)=I(x,y,t)$; linearising gives the optical
    flow constraint $I_x u + I_y v + I_t = 0$.
  \item \textbf{Small motion:} the displacement is small enough for that first-order
    Taylor expansion to hold. The image pyramid extends this to larger motions, coarse to
    fine.
  \item \textbf{Spatial coherence:} all pixels in the window around a feature share the
    same translational flow, which gives an over-determined system solved by least
    squares. The system is well conditioned only if the $2\times2$ structure tensor
    $\sum\nabla I\nabla I^\top$ has two large eigenvalues, and that is exactly what the
    Harris detector selects; hence the pairing of Harris corners with LK.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Comparison with the descriptor-based methods.}
Harris+LK has the highest inlier ratio on both datasets (99.7\,\% and 95.5\,\%), is among
the fastest (3.0 and 5.9\,ms per pair), and its inlier count (552 and 255) is comparable
to ORB and FAST+BRIEF. It is fast because it computes no descriptors, searches locally
and reuses the corners of the previous frame. It is reliable because LK searches for
each feature only around its previous position, so it cannot produce the distant
ambiguous matches that a global descriptor search can (between repeated gates or floor
tiles, for example), and tracking only inliers keeps bad points from propagating. In
the per-frame plots (Fig.~\ref{fig:perframe}) LK has the steadiest curve, while the
descriptor methods dip at fast rotations. The distributions in
Fig.~\ref{fig:ratiodist} show the same: the 5th percentile of the LK inlier ratio is
98.7\,\% on the real and 88.2\,\% on the simulated data, against 86.4\,\% and 75.6\,\% for
SIFT. Its strength comes from its assumptions: LK
needs small inter-frame motion and constant brightness, and long tracks accumulate
drift. Matching unrelated views, as in the box pair of Deliverable~6.a, loop closure or
re-localisation, remains the job of descriptors.

\subsection{Deliverable 8 (optional): Farneback dense optical flow}\label{sec:flow}

The node in \code{self\_flow.cpp} computes the dense flow between consecutive grayscale
frames with Farneback's polynomial-expansion method~\cite{farneback2003flow}
(\code{calcOpticalFlowFarneback}; pyramid scale 0.5, 3 levels, $15\times15$ window, 3
iterations, polynomial neighbourhood 5, $\sigma=1.2$). It shows, side by side, the frame
with the flow vectors on a 16-pixel grid and a colour-coded flow field (hue = direction,
brightness = magnitude, saturated at 10\,px). We ran it on both datasets
(\code{roslaunch lab\_5} \code{self\_flow.launch}).
